A Culture Where Leaders Make the Call

Culture is how decisions get made and how people keep their word. Your calendar shows where your time goes and where decisions really happen.

Michael PelusoFounder, True Living · 5 min read

The culture you want is one where capable leaders make the calls in their area, keep their word and grow with the company. The clearest record of the culture you have is the calendar. It shows where time goes and where decisions really get made. If most of them happen in one-off calls to the CEO, the culture has been built around one seat, however strong the rest of the team is.

Values on the wall are a start. They become culture when they show up at 7 a.m. on a Monday, when a job is behind and two project managers need the same crew.

Culture is how decisions get made

At the heart of a company's culture are the leaders' actions and the way decisions get made. That shapes how every person on the team works and how they go about solving problems.

In a lot of companies, the decision process was never designed. It grew up around the founder. A problem comes up, someone calls the founder, the founder decides. That works at $2M, and it's how most good companies get started. By $8M the company has outgrown it. Capable people have more judgment to offer than the structure lets them use, and the best of them want room to use it.

It also makes the decision process the slowest link in the company. When every call routes through one seat, the whole company moves at the speed of one calendar. In Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, that's the bottleneck, and it's the first thing to fix. We explain how in At $5M, the Bottleneck Is Usually Structure, Not People.

If you want a culture where people solve problems where they find them and leaders own their results, the decision process has to give them room to do it.

What your calendar says right now

Pull up the last month. Look at two things.

First, the CEO's time. Mark every block as work only the CEO can do, work a leader could own, or interruption. Most CEOs find the first category is small. That's the company's most valuable time, and it deserves more room.

Then look at every recurring meeting on the company calendar and ask three questions about each one.

  • Does it end with decisions, each with an owner and a date?
  • Does anyone check the next week whether those things got done?
  • If it disappeared tomorrow, would anyone miss a decision?

Many companies we walk into have a Monday huddle that has turned into a status report, a few site walks, and everything else handled by phone. Meetings that don't decide anything teach people that real decisions happen somewhere else, in side conversations and one-off calls.

Adding meetings won't fix that. Cut the ones that don't decide anything. Keep the few that do, and make them count.

A rhythm that protects your time

Time is the first part of the TEAM System, the method inside True Living OS. It frees the CEO's time for what grows the company: vision, key relationships and the next big move. Your time freedom isn't a perk. The company's next level depends on it. Our team maps where that time goes, protects the work only the CEO can do, and builds a rhythm that puts the rest of the decisions with the leaders who should own them.

The rhythm is simple.

  • The CEO's protected time. Blocks for the work only the CEO can do. Pricing the big bids, key relationships, banking, developing your leaders, where the company goes next.
  • A weekly decision session for your leaders. Same day, same time. Scorecard first, then the issues list in priority order. Issues get decided in the room, and every decision leaves with an owner and a due date. AI agents gather what the session needs and follow up after it.
  • A monthly business review. You and your leaders look at the scorecard trend and only the decisions that sit above the limits you've set.
  • A quarterly session. A longer working session to step back and set the next quarter's priorities, on site or on an Executive Retreat.

The same rhythm is how change sticks. When you want to change something, like how estimates get reviewed or how closeout gets handled, it gets introduced, tracked and checked in the rhythm until it's simply how the company works. A change announced in a memo fades fast. A change reviewed every week sticks.

Agreements make the rhythm work

A rhythm runs on agreements. We use a short set called the True Living agreements, and four of them carry most of the weight in a leadership team.

  • Be on time. Start on time and come prepared. If the scorecard isn't filled in ahead of time, the session can't do its job.
  • Honor your word. Do what you said you'd do by when you said you'd do it. If you can't, say so before the deadline.
  • Take full responsibility. Own the result, including the parts that went sideways.
  • Practice open communication. Raise the problem in the room, where it can get solved. Give honest feedback and be willing to take it.

These sound basic. Most of the friction we see between leaders traces back to one of them being broken.

Put people where they do their best work

Culture also depends on who is doing what. Every person brings a natural way of solving problems that goes beyond their job description. A superintendent who's great at sequencing a job may hate running a meeting. A project manager who's great with customers may struggle with cost tracking.

That's why our team starts by meeting every member of yours and seeing where each leader is strongest and ready for more. When people work in line with how they naturally operate, the company moves with less friction. When they're in roles that fight their nature, everyone works harder for less.

Structure buys freedom

Owners sometimes worry that structure will slow them down. In practice it frees up the week for everyone. When decisions have a home, they get made on time, by the right person. Your leaders know where they stand and what they own, with the numbers to back every call. The CEO gets time back for the next big move, and the company keeps moving while the CEO is away.
