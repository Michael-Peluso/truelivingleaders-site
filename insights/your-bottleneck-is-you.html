At $5M, the Bottleneck Is Usually Structure, Not People

Every company is a chain, and its slowest link sets the pace. Past $5M, that link is usually the way decisions are structured, not the people making them.

Michael PelusoFounder, True Living · 6 min read

In most companies doing $5M to $20M a year, the thing that limits growth isn't effort or talent. It's structure. The company grew faster than the way it makes decisions, so pricing, change orders and customer calls still route through one seat, usually the CEO's. Your leaders are capable and ready for more authority. The structure just hasn't caught up with them.

Fix the structure and the whole company speeds up. The first step is seeing it clearly, and the clearest way to see it is the Theory of Constraints.

A business is a chain

Eliyahu Goldratt laid out the Theory of Constraints in The Goal, a novel about a plant manager trying to save his factory. The idea is simple enough to draw on a napkin.

Every business is a chain of steps. In a contracting company the chain looks like this. You win the work. You estimate and sell it. You deliver it. You collect the money. Every company has its own version, and people and decisions hold every link together.

The chain can only move as fast as its slowest link. Goldratt called that link the bottleneck. Improve any other link and output barely moves, because the work still has to squeeze through the slow one. Improve the bottleneck and the whole chain speeds up.

You already know this from the job site. If the pump truck is two hours late, it doesn't matter how many finishers are standing around. The pour starts when the pump shows up. Work piles up in front of the bottleneck, and everything downstream stalls.

The first question our team asks in any company is the one Goldratt would ask. Where does work pile up? In a lot of $5M+ companies, it piles up wherever decisions still route to one seat.

How to tell the structure is the bottleneck

Look at where work piles up. The same signs show up in almost every growing company we walk into.

  • The CEO's week goes to day-to-day calls instead of customers, banking and the next big move.
  • There's no written dollar limit on bids, so every bid gets the same final review.
  • Change orders get done in the field and priced weeks later, because pricing authority was never handed off.
  • Field questions travel up the chain by text, because there's no set time or place to decide them.
  • Your leaders are capable and ready for more, and nobody has written down what they can decide on their own.
  • A week away for the CEO slows the whole company down.

None of this means anyone did anything wrong. You can have a strong leadership team and still have this bottleneck. The company was built around its founder. In the early years one person made every call, and that was the right structure for the time. As revenue grew, the structure stayed the same. Every new job added decisions, and every decision still routes through the same seat.

Growth makes it tighter. More jobs means more decisions stacked in front of the same seat. I own and operate construction businesses myself, so I know how easily a company outgrows the structure that built it without anyone noticing.

If you want a quick read on where your company's time is tied up, the Growth Check asks six questions, one for each part of the chain, and takes about two minutes.

The five focusing steps, applied to a leadership team

Goldratt's method has five steps. He called them the five focusing steps, and they translate directly when the bottleneck is the way a company decides.

1. Identify the bottleneck. For two weeks, write down every decision that reaches the CEO. Note where it came from, what it was and whether a leader could have made it with clear authority and the right numbers. When CEOs do this, the list is almost always longer than they expected, and much of it belongs with leaders who are ready for it.

2. Exploit the bottleneck. Get the most out of the bottleneck before you add anything. When decisions route through the CEO, that means treating the CEO's time as the company's most valuable asset and protecting it for the work only the CEO can do. Pricing bids above a certain size. Key customer relationships. Banking and bonding. Developing the leadership team. Deciding where the company goes next. Everything else gets batched so it reaches the CEO once, at a set time.

3. Subordinate everything else. Line the rest of the company up behind the bottleneck. Decisions that need the CEO arrive with the facts and a recommendation ready. A weekly scorecard shows every leader where things stand, so nobody has to chase updates. Nothing reaches the CEO that the system could have handled.

4. Elevate the bottleneck. Add capacity. In a company, decision-making capacity comes from people, and most of it is already on the team. Write down who decides what, and up to what dollar amount. Give each leader ownership of their area, their numbers and their decisions, with the support to grow into more. Put the routine work into written processes so fewer decisions come up at all.

5. Repeat. Once decisions stop routing through one seat, the bottleneck moves. It might move to estimating capacity, field crews, cash or the front of your sales funnel. That's the problem you want. It means the company can grow past the limit of any one person's hours. Goldratt added one warning to this step. Don't let inertia become the bottleneck. The fix that worked last year can be the thing holding you back this year.

Then the bottleneck moves

This is the part most companies miss. Clearing the bottleneck is a cycle, and the cycle keeps running.

Our team works it as a four-part process. Map is identify. Design covers exploit and subordinate, where we build the scorecard, the lanes, the decision rights and the rhythm around the CEO's time. Install is elevate, where your leaders take full ownership of their lanes and the AI agents take on the gathering and follow-up. Grow is repeat. The company grows, the bottleneck moves, and we go again.

What gets installed is a management ecosystem. Clear numbers, leaders with clear lanes, a rhythm for decisions, written processes and AI agents that keep it all moving. Each part feeds the others. Together they let the company run and grow without every decision routing through one seat. Our version of it is True Living OS.

Why it pays twice

Fixing the structure frees the CEO's time for vision, key relationships and the next big move. For the CFO, the COO and the rest of the leadership team, it means clear authority, real-time numbers every week and a company that keeps moving when any one person is away. It also makes the company worth more. A company that depends on any one person is a job that pays well. A company that runs on a system, with leaders who own the calls, is an asset an investor would pay for.

One owner we worked with ran a commercial contracting company. His CFO told him it would take ten years before he could step back. He was out of the day-to-day years ahead of that forecast. With his time back, he set a bigger goal and moved into land development.

I also advised, and held equity in, a residential concrete company that grew more than sevenfold, from one subcontractor to six salespeople, an office team, a new building and three locations. A company doesn't make that climb with every decision routed through one seat.

We won't promise a revenue number or a date. Results depend on you and your team doing the work. We can tell you the bottleneck can be found, measured and moved.

Where to start this week

Run the two-week decision log. At the end, sort it into three piles. Decisions only the CEO should make. Decisions a leader could own today. Decisions a leader could own with clear limits and the right numbers.

Hand off the second pile right away. The third pile is your plan for taking the leadership team to the next level. The first pile is the CEO's job. Protect the time for it, because the company's next level depends on it.
