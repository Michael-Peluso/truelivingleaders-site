Case study · Contractor

Map the work first. Then build the software.

At one contractor, we mapped all 97 steps of a job, then built one system on that map, from first call to warranty. Five short videos show how it works.

Every screen runs on sample data made up for these videos. The client’s name, people, jobs and numbers aren’t shown, and a few scenes are drawn to show a feature rather than captured from the live system.

One system, first call to warranty

The sales spreadsheet counted the losses, because somebody calls to tell you no. The wins got talked about in meetings and never typed in. Estimating had its own spreadsheet, and the cash forecast lived on a dry-erase board.

Before we built a single screen, we mapped all 97 steps of a job and walked a test job through every one. It broke at the handoffs, sales to estimating and contract to job, not at the people. The system we built on that map runs from first call to warranty, on the Google Workspace the company already had. A test job ran through it from new lead to archived: 84 checks, no errors.

Read the script

Does your win rate count the jobs you won?

At one contractor, the sales spreadsheet didn't.

Losses got entered, because somebody calls to tell you no.

Wins got talked about in meetings and never typed in.

Estimating had its own spreadsheet.

The cash forecast lived on a dry-erase board.

So we didn't start with software.

With the leadership team, we mapped all 97 steps of a job, who does each one, and where its record lives.

We walked a test job through every step.

It broke between sales and estimating, and again between contract and job.

Good people.

The handoffs between them were the problem.

We built one system on that map, first call to warranty.

Sign a deal, and one hand-off screen turns the estimate into the budget, buyout, schedule of values, and job schedule.

Nothing gets typed twice.

The super's daily report works with no signal and sends itself when service returns.

The building owner approves change orders online, without seeing the contractor's cost.

Leadership gets a weekly scorecard and a cash forecast by month or quarter.

Built with AI, on the Google Workspace the company already had.

No new per-seat subscription.

When a team has a map to point at, they stop pointing at each other and fix the system.

That's when people step up.

That's how you build a company that lasts for generations.

Inside the system

Four parts, one at a time.

1:06

The Sales Toolbox

One page for before, during and after every customer meeting. The whole sale sits on the rep’s phone: six steps, what to ask at each one, and fourteen ready answers for pushback. Nobody types a to-do. Each one shows up because of something the rep already did, and clears when it’s done.

Read the script

What's your rep looking at in the truck, two minutes before the meeting?

At one contractor, we gave the reps one page for before, during, and after every customer meeting.

The whole sale is one strip of six steps.

Tap the step you're on, and it shows the goal, what to ask and say, what to watch for, and what to leave with.

And it changes with how the customer came in: an idea, drawings over budget, or finished drawings to price.

Tick off the questions, and when the customer pushes back, there are fourteen ready answers.

After the meeting, nobody types a to-do.

Book a meeting or set a touch date, and the to-do appears on its own.

Do it, and it's gone.

Missed meetings, links not sent, the customer's lender or architect due a call: sorted into overdue, today, this week, and later.

It's built for a phone at a job site, and works on a weak signal once it's loaded.

Every update lands on the same link, so reps never get a new one.

A sale that's written down can be taught.

That's how a sales team grows.

1:10

Scope gaps

What happens in the first hour after a super finds work in nobody’s scope? We wrote that hour down as a procedure and built it into the software: stop, log it, tell the PM, and find whose gap it is before anyone talks about a fix. Every step sits on the PM’s screen, with who to tell and by when.

Read the script

When your super finds work in nobody's scope, what happens in the next hour?

At one contractor, we wrote the answer down, then built it into their software.

First rule: stop.

Don't do the work, don't tell a sub to do it, and don't OK labor or material until it's approved.

The super logs it from the site, down to the grid line.

It gets its own number, and the clock starts.

The PM hears within the hour, the senior PM and the estimator within four.

From there, the PM owns it.

The PM's screen shows every step, who to tell, and by when.

Before anyone talks about a fix, find out whose gap it is.

Check the drawings, specs and addenda, the contractor's scope and the sub's, and any direction given in the field.

Past a set dollar amount, the CFO hears about it.

Every fix gets a cost review, and approval goes by dollar limit.

If it's on the owner's side, it becomes a change order.

Either way, it closes with the actual cost.

When the super who finds a gap doesn't carry it alone, people raise their hand early.

1:10

Leadership meetings

Every management meeting on one calendar, from the weekly sales meeting to each job’s OAC. Every action item gets a name and a due date, lands on that person’s to-do list, and carries into the next meeting if it isn’t done. The team page is a circle of seats around the company’s why, not a pyramid.

Read the script

What happened to the action items from last Monday's meeting?

At one contractor, we put every management meeting on one calendar.

Weekly sales and ops, monthly finance and executive, quarterly all-hands and safety, and every job's OAC.

Tap a date, and the meeting opens with its agenda and live numbers.

Notes go under each agenda item, and minutes can go to the owner.

Every action item gets a name and a due date, and lands on that person's to-do list.

Whatever isn't done carries into the next meeting.

The team page starts with the company's why, then the ten-year target and this year's plan.

The team itself is a circle of seats around that why, not a pyramid.

Tap a person, and their five roles come up.

Ninety-day sprint goals, company and personal, on track or off.

The weekly scorecard fills in where it can, each number with a name and a target.

Departments write down what they owe each other, and by when.

Every pair starts blank and fills it in together.

That's how a company gets built to last, one week at a time.

1:10

Field to owner

No logins, just links. The super talks the daily log in, every sub confirms their own dates, the architect answers RFIs, and the building owner approves change orders, each from their own page. The owner sees progress and the photos the team chooses to share. The contractor’s cost never shows.

Read the script

How many phone calls does it take to find out what happened on site today?

At one contractor, we gave everyone outside the office their own link, no login.

The super gets the two-week lookahead, the daily log, punch, and RFIs.

Tap “Talk it in,” say what happened, and it lands on today's log for the PM.

With no signal, the log waits and sends itself when the signal's back.

Every sub gets one link: every job they're on, ten weeks out.

They confirm the dates, or ask for new ones with a reason.

The PM sees what else moves and records who the delay is on.

Then one tap moves the schedule and flags the trades to re-confirm.

The architect answers RFIs from a link, and an answer that costs money starts a change order.

The building owner gets a page: percent complete, what's under way, and only the photos the team shares.

Change orders get approved with a check box and a typed name, and land on the next pay app.

The contractor's cost never shows.

When everyone owns their piece, the whole team steps up.
