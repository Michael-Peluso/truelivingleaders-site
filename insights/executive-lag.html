Executive Lag: How Fast Your Company Decides

Executive lag is the time between a problem being raised and a decision being made. Shrinking it is one of the fastest ways a company grows.

Michael PelusoFounder, True Living · 4 min read

Executive lag is the time between an issue being raised and a decision being made. Shrink it, and jobs move faster, margin holds and every leader gets more room to decide. Past $5M, most lag comes from structure. The company grew faster than the way it decides, so too many calls still route through one seat. You can measure it, and once you measure it, you can cut it.

We put it on the scorecard because every leadership team feels it and almost nobody tracks it. It's also the clearest number we know for spotting the bottleneck. When decisions queue up in front of one seat, the way the company decides has become its slowest link.

What it looks like on a job

A superintendent hits an unforeseen condition on Tuesday morning. He calls the project manager. The PM knows it's a change order and knows what it should cost, but pricing on this customer has always been the CEO's call. The CEO is in a bid review and then on another site, sees the message that night and asks for photos on Wednesday. The PM sends them Thursday. The price is approved Friday.

The crew worked around the problem for three days. The customer got the change order a week after the work started and pushed back on it. A capable PM spent the week on a decision he was ready to make.

Nobody in that story did anything wrong. The structure sent the decision to one seat, and that seat was busy with work only the CEO can do. That gap is executive lag, and it ran from Tuesday to Friday on a decision that could have taken an hour.

Why it stays hidden

Executive lag hides inside other problems. Schedule slips. Change orders written down or written off. Overtime to catch up. Customers who feel ignored. Good people who leave because they're ready for more authority than the structure gives them.

It also feels like work from the CEO's chair. The day is full of decisions, so it looks like the company is being managed. Meanwhile the queue of decisions in front of one seat keeps getting longer.

How to measure it

Start a simple log and keep it for a month. Every issue that needs a decision gets one line with four entries.

  1. The date it was raised, and who raised it.
  2. What the issue was.
  3. Who made the decision.
  4. The date the decision was made.

Subtract the dates and you have the lag on each issue. Then sort the list. Sort by who decided, and see how many landed on one desk. Sort by type, and see which issues take longest. In most companies a few types make up most of the list. Pricing calls, schedule conflicts between jobs, hiring and customer escalations are common ones.

Pick one number for the scorecard. Average days from issue raised to decision made works fine. Your leadership team reviews it every week and watches which way it moves.

AI makes this easier to keep up. When your leadership sessions are recorded and transcribed, an agent can pull out every issue raised and every decision made, with an owner and a date. The log builds itself, and lag becomes a number you can see without anyone doing data entry.

How to cut it

Lag comes down when every decision has a place to go and a person with the authority to make it.

Write down who decides what. Put dollar limits on it, and revisit them each quarter. A project manager can approve change orders up to an amount you set. An estimator can adjust markup inside a range. Anything above the line comes to the CEO. A good part of the queue usually clears once this is written down, because your leaders often already knew the right answer. The structure just never gave them the authority.

Give decisions a set time and place. Most decisions can wait a few days if everyone knows exactly when they'll be made. Your leaders work the issues list in priority order on a fixed day each week, and every decision leaves with an owner and a due date. Anything that truly can't wait has a clear path, and everyone knows what it is. The point is fewer interruptions for everyone, more protected time for the CEO and fewer standing meetings that never decide anything.

Map the swimlanes. A swimlane map shows how information and responsibility move through the company, from the first sale to the final invoice. When you draw it, you see every handoff that routes through one seat for no good reason. Then you redraw it.

Back the people who decide. Authority on paper works best when leaders have the numbers and the support to use it. Our team works with the CEO and up to two key leaders every week. Much of that time goes to helping leaders take full ownership of the calls in their area and make them with confidence.

Let AI handle the follow-through. Agents can gather the scorecard numbers ahead of time, draft the issues list from the week's emails and notes, and send each person their decisions and due dates the same day. Fewer decisions get lost between conversations.

What changes when lag comes down

Decisions start happening at the speed of the job. Change orders get priced while the work is fresh, and margin holds. Your leaders get even better at deciding because they do it every week. The CEO's time goes to vision, key relationships and the next big move, and the decisions that still reach the CEO are the ones that should.

This is the A in the TEAM System, the method inside True Living OS. AI brings consistency, process and feedback loops to the way a company decides. Agents gather the updates, track the numbers and follow through, so execution gets faster and more consistent and the gap between a problem and a decision keeps shrinking. Once that gap is small, the company is ready for the M, Momentum, and it is time to step on the gas.
